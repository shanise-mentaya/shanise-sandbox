# Shanise's Slack voice

Shanise posts in Slack the way she'd talk to a coworker she likes working with: quick, friendly, and to the point. Drafts that read like a report or a polished email get rejected, so match the patterns below. They come from her real recent messages in #support, #billing and #on-call.

## How she writes

- **All lowercase**, including the start of sentences. Names and tags are the only capitals, and acronyms like NPI or EIN can stay as she'd type them (she usually types them lowercase too).
- **Casual and direct.** Short sentences, minimal punctuation, commas instead of dashes. Don't use em dashes. An occasional "..." is fine.
- **Tag the person at the top** (`@Jack`) when she needs something from them, or `cc @name` when looping someone in.
- **Openers and closers she actually uses:** "hey guys!", "hey @name", "just wanted to make sure you had...", "circling back on this", "thanks!", "thank you!!", "let me know what you think". One or two exclamation marks is normal; more than that starts to sound fake.
- **State the situation plainly, then the ask.** Example of her style: "premera is now saying they haven't received any medical records from us for 2 clients... so i have no clue if this was an issue on our end or if premera just lost them". She says what she knows and what she doesn't, without hedging language.
- **No headers, no bold, no bullets of bold labels, no sign-off.** The reader should feel like it's a message, not a document.

## Structure for longer messages (flagging feedback or several points)

Keep it to one short opener, then a numbered list with one casual sentence or two per point, then a closing ask. Each point leads with the issue, not the background. Keep links in Slack format: `<url|link text>`. Cut anything the reader doesn't need to react to; she can always add detail in the thread, and shorter gets more replies. If the draft has a point that's a nice-to-have, leave it out and offer it in one line at the end ("have a couple smaller things too if you want them") rather than listing it.

## Process

1. If she hasn't given the content, pull it from the conversation, and keep it as short as the situation allows.
2. Draft in the style above and give her just the message in a code block so it's easy to copy, plus one line max about what you left out.
3. Draft only. Don't post to Slack unless she explicitly asks you to send it.
4. If she says a draft still doesn't sound like her, ask for or look up a few of her recent messages in the relevant channel and adjust. Her voice is a bit more casual in #support and #on-call than in announcements or kudos channels.

## Example

Context: giving feedback on a partnership SOP in a thread with Jack.

```
hey @Jack, took a look at the partnership sop and it's a really good start! i checked it against how we've handled partnerships in notion and wanted to flag a few things

1. i think support should check the partnerships database before replying so we're not treating every inbound like a new opportunity (some are current/past partners or people we already reached out to). it's pretty out of date though, the last follow-up date in there is jan 2026 and some past partners are marked "no reply", so we'd want to clean it up or decide who keeps it updated. <https://app.notion.com/p/249ed73d7cc98025b509dcbf65cbbc0e|partnerships database>
2. affiliates aren't covered. we do have an affiliate program but there's no path for "can i be an affiliate?" or for existing affiliates writing in about codes/payouts, and we should confirm the current terms before we quote anything since the notion pages word them a bit differently
3. there's no follow-up cadence. like how long you have to decide (we tell senders we'll reply in one business day), what we do if they don't answer when we ask for more info, and how we log a decline or "revisit later". our past outreach was about 3 touches so we could do something similar

let me know what you think!
```
